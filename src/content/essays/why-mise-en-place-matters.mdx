In culinary school, mise en place—French for "everything in its place"—is treated as sacred. You prep every ingredient, measure every spice, and arrange everything in little bowls before you turn on a burner. The idea is simple: with everything ready, you can focus entirely on cooking.

Then I started cooking at home and had to do my own dishes.

## The Professional Reality

In a restaurant kitchen, mise en place isn't optional. When you're firing thirty covers during a rush, you don't have time to mince shallots. Everything must be within arm's reach, portioned, and ready to go. A missing ingredient doesn't just slow you down—it can crash your whole station.

But professional kitchens have prep cooks. Someone else chops those onions. Someone else portions the butter. The line cook who sautés your salmon never touched a cutting board.

## The Home Cook Reality

When Nick and I first cooked together, I instinctively started prepping everything before we began. He looked at me like I was wasting time.

"Why are you putting salt in a tiny bowl? It's salt. Just grab it when you need it."

He had a point. Some aspects of mise en place are pure habit, carried over from professional environments where they make sense but don't translate to home cooking.

## Where Nick Is Right

**You don't need everything in cute little bowls.** If you're sautéing onions and the next step is adding garlic, you can mince the garlic while the onions cook. That's not cutting corners—that's efficient time management.

**Some ingredients can be prepped on the fly.** If a recipe calls for "juice of one lemon," you can juice that lemon while something simmers. You don't need pre-juiced lemon waiting in a ramekin.

**Dishes matter.** Professional mise en place generates a mountain of prep containers. At home, that's your problem. Fewer dishes often means a better cooking experience.

## Where I'm Still Right

**Time-sensitive techniques need full attention.** If you're searing a steak, you need that butter and thyme ready immediately. You can't be digging through your spice cabinet while your $30 ribeye overcooks.

**Complicated dishes benefit from organization.** A stir-fry, where everything cooks in seconds, genuinely needs ingredients prepped and staged. Same with anything involving deglazing, where the pan is screaming hot and timing matters.

**Reading the full recipe first is non-negotiable.** This isn't technically mise en place, but the principle applies: know what's coming before you start. The worst surprise is discovering you need "rested, chilled dough" when you're about to start rolling.

## The Compromise

After years of cooking together, Nick and I have landed somewhere in the middle. I still prep more than he does—old habits—but I've relaxed about the bowl situation. He's learned to have time-sensitive ingredients ready before the heat goes on.

The goal isn't Instagram-worthy bowls of prepped ingredients. It's cooking without stress, without scrambling, without that moment of "oh no, where's the—"

Mise en place is a tool. Like any tool, it should serve you, not the other way around.

## The Practical Version

Here's how I'd adapt mise en place for home cooking:

1. **Read the recipe fully** before you start. Note any time-sensitive moments.

2. **Pre-prep only what's time-critical.** If you need to add five ingredients simultaneously, have those ready. If they're added over ten minutes of simmering, prep as you go.

3. **Group ingredients logically.** Pull everything from the fridge and pantry before starting. Even if you're not pre-chopping, knowing where everything is matters.

4. **Set up your workspace.** Clean cutting board, sharp knife, salt within reach, trash bowl nearby. This is mise en place at its core—organization that makes cooking flow.

5. **Accept imperfection.** Sometimes you'll realize mid-cook that you forgot something. It happens. The dish will probably still be good.

The little bowls are optional. The mindset of being prepared is not.
